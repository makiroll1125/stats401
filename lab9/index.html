Lab 9: Geospatial Visualization

Two views of 2025 nominal GDP for the 50 largest economies. Hover over a country in either map to highlight it in both views. Click to keep it selected; click again or press Escape to clear.

1. Dataset Description

The supplied CSV lists 2025 nominal GDP in billions of current U.S. dollars, plus GDP rank and ISO-3 identifier. It is based on the IMF World Economic Outlook, October 2025. WEO values can include staff estimates and may be revised. Country boundaries come from Natural Earth; the data join uses the map's ISO-3 administration code. Economies outside the top 50 have no GDP value in this dataset.

2. 2025 GDP Choropleth

Country shape shows geographic boundaries. Color shows nominal GDP. Scroll or pinch to zoom, then drag to pan.

The color scale is logarithmic. GDP ranges from about $300 billion to $30.6 trillion, so a linear scale would make most economies look nearly alike. The log scale gives more visible color differences across the full range; legend labels show actual GDP values.

3. 2025 GDP Cartogram

Country polygon area represents 2025 GDP for the top 50 economies. This contiguous cartogram distorts a world map using the Cartogram Chart example. Neutral countries have no GDP value in the CSV. A small display weight keeps most neutral boundaries visible; 24 tiny no-data areas are omitted at this resolution. Their display weight and area are not GDP estimates.

4. Design and Comparison

On the choropleth, the United States, China, and Russia occupy much of the map because their territories are large. Dark color identifies the United States and China as the largest GDPs, while Russia and Canada have large geographic footprints despite smaller GDPs. Germany, Japan, and the United Kingdom can be easy to overlook at world scale, even with dark fills.

In the cartogram, the United States and China expand strongly. Germany, Japan, India, and the United Kingdom gain space relative to their ordinary map footprints; Russia, Canada, and Australia contract. The choropleth preserves familiar borders, coastlines, neighboring countries, and approximate distances. It supports locating economies and scanning regional patterns, although small wealthy countries remain hard to see and colors are imprecise for ranking. The cartogram uses distorted polygon area to make economic magnitude easier to judge while keeping a recognizable world layout. Its stretched borders and moved coastlines make distance, land area, and exact shape unreliable. Linked highlighting helps identify a distorted country in the geographic map. Encoding GDP through area makes the concentration of output in the United States and China more immediate than color alone, but it reduces geographic precision.
